📄 原文全文

¶1Two out of three Americans bring back a souvenir from their travels, with US sales topping $21 billion in 2022. What does it really mean to take home a piece of a place, and at what cost? Humans have always had a passion for collecting things. The Romans brought home spices, animal skins and rare handcrafts to commemorate their travels and conquests. In the 18th century, places like Rome's Colosseum were damaged by souvenir-seekers. With the rise of industrialisation and capitalism, a lot changed: the world became more connected, tourism boomed and profits took centre stage. But the human need to remember stayed. "Looking at a souvenir can bring back happy emotions as the brain links it to positive experiences," said Vaishnavi Madarkal, a psychologist and therapist based in India. "This connection can happen with anything -- whether it's an object, music or a smell."

¶2But how does this fit into a world already overwhelmed by excess? "In Europe and America, protests against overtourism are growing. People are realising their vacations can leave lasting impacts on others' homes," says Justin Francis, specialising in ethical tourism. Still, a souvenir remains to be an often-overlooked side of tourism's impact. In a world flooded with mass-produced items and fake markets, it is crucial to examine products, and ask, 'Where did this come from?' A 2022 report by the Australian Government found that up to 75% of souvenirs marketed as "indigenous" in Australia were fake.

¶3The battle of pashmina shawl artisans against cheaper, mass-produced alternatives is also well known. "In the Leh market in Ladakh, India, about 95% of fabrics sold as pashmina aren't genuine," says Sonam Angmo. She is co-founder of Lena Ladakh Pashmina, a slow textile brand focused on preserving the pashmina legacy while supporting local artisans. "The fake fabric is mass-produced in places like Ludhiana and Punjab then brought to Ladakh by outsiders." A genuine pashmina shawl can cost upwards of 30,000 rupees ($375) due to the effort of handcrafting the shawls. The high cost of genuine pashmina has driven demand for imitations, with the profitability of fake or "semi-pashmina" ensuring a steady supply. Most customers simply seek a pashmina "bought in Ladakh" and give little regard to its origins. Angmo rightly calls this a "vicious cycle" that impacts local communities dependent on tourism. Frustrated by the difficulty of selling authentic items, some artisans resort to purchasing fake fabrics or working with machines. "It's how they feed their families," Angmo explains. This may create a dilemma for shoppers: while purchasing fake goods might seem to support local retailers, the true cost is often paid by artisans who rely on traditional craftsmanship.

¶4One thing is clear: the desire to immortalise our travels isn't going anywhere. What's more, many communities with busy tourist sites have flourished by turning souvenirs into a source of income. Perhaps it's not about avoiding souvenirs entirely, but about making responsible choices. It used to be easier to spot genuine local souvenirs. But today's imitations are so sophisticated that even experts may struggle to distinguish them. Meanwhile, sellers are more aware of consumer mindfulness, often marketing items as "handcrafted" or "recycled," though the truth can be far from the label.

¶5So, how can you buy souvenirs in an ethical, sustainable and meaningful way? "Be curious. It's not just about buying or choosing carefully, it's about rejecting and explaining why," said Francis. "You could say, 'This sculpture is beautiful, but I'm concerned about its environmental impact.'" Another way to ethically shop is to support fair-trade markets. Many countries now certify authentic local goods, too. In India, genuine pashmina shawls bear GI (Geographical Indication) tags. Intentions also matter. I no longer buy souvenirs on every trip. When I do, I ensure they're meaningful. Sometimes, I even ask friends what they want instead of guessing.

¶6A good souvenir doesn't always have to be bought. When you think beyond "souvenirs" and more about what sparks memories, the possibilities get exciting. Maybe it's a letter sent from your destination, a well-kept travel journal, or the sounds of their adventures. Ultimately, there's no universal rule for what makes a "responsible" souvenir; it's a personal decision we each make. But next time you're checking your gift list, pause and reflect.

¶1It all started about three years ago, when I met up with my son in a park. He's a very outdoor type and it was the first time I'd seen him barefoot -- he likes the [dimension] it feels on grass, but doesn't do it all the time. I found it funny, so I took my shoes off to give it a go. It was as though I'd discovered an extra sense. Afterwards, I wanted to keep it up, but not only in parks. I'm an all-or-nothing kind of guy.

¶2The plan was to go without shoes for as long as I could, at least from spring until mid-October when it got too cold. But we had a very mild autumn last year and I love a challenge, so I thought: Can I keep going?

¶3The snow last December was the first big test. I covered my feet in vaseline to keep warm; it's what cold-water swimmers do in winter. It worked, provided I didn't stay still for long. Then we had frost. I'd wear gloves and all these layers, but no shoes, and again found it was fine as long as I kept moving.

¶4I've been barefoot in London every day since March last year. Living in a city, I love the element of the unexpected. Travelling on the train barefoot was novel at first because there are many interesting surfaces such as raised circles on the smooth platforms. It's as if I've reached a new [dimension] most people normally never experience.

¶5I grew up in south-east England before joining the railways in the 1970s and training to be a mechanic. I took early retirement with a good pension in 2009. These days, I do a lot of community work. When I was delivering medicine across south-east London during the pandemic, I went to a 1950s apartment block. They'd freshly surfaced all the walking paths, which felt absolutely amazing on my bare feet. It was like walking across very fine sandpaper.

¶6I'm a volunteer for a community garden. Being outside, being barefoot, helping people -- I'm sure it's all connected. I've been diagnosed with depression, but I discovered that being involved with the community, and being in nature really assisted with my recovery.

¶7I bring a pair of slippers with me everywhere in [case] I need to put them on. Ridiculously, I'm permitted to go shoeless in Forest Hill Library but not Lewisham Library due to "health and safety," even though they're run by the same local authority. Most independently owned places have no issue with people being barefoot, but one of the popular pub chains doesn't allow it. The only time I properly wear footwear is when I go clubbing -- I regularly go to a major nightclub in London -- because security insists. When I take my shoes off at the end of the night, my feet feel as if they're on fire.

¶8I have always worn a lot of bright colours -- that gets me a lot of attention as a 64-year-old man anyway, but I could write a book about the range of reactions I get about my feet. Some people get it, others less so. The most common question is: "Where are your shoes?"

¶9Four people offered me shoes in the space of a week during the last bit of cold weather. I always stay calm and tell them it's a lifestyle [choice]. It certainly starts interesting conversations and at least people are curious, even if they think it's strange behaviour.

¶10I have to care for my feet because, although they've become tough, the skin does crack. I'm still trying to find the best way to look after them: I use antiseptic, skin cream and lard to help prevent the soles from breaking. The worst [injury] I had was from stepping on a one-and-a-half-inch nail while gardening. There are also some challenging surfaces which are painful to stand on but satisfying to get over.

¶11Being barefoot makes me feel more emotionally stable and helps with my well-being. Hardly anyone else seems to have an idea how incredible it feels and it's most interesting when done in a city. (I'll never go back to wearing shoes again.)
